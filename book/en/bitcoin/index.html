Proof of Work

Why Bitcoin matters

Money is becoming a surveillance tool

Almost every payment you make is now digital. Each one is tied to your identity, stored for years, and analysed: by your bank, by the card network, by the payment app, sometimes by the shop. A list of your payments says where you were, what you read, which doctor you saw, which cause you support. It is a diary you never decided to write.

Identity checks, known as KYC ("know your customer"), now stand at the entrance of almost every financial service. The copies of your passport they collect are stored in many places, and some of them leak.

A payment that passes through an intermediary can also be refused by that intermediary. Accounts get frozen by mistake, by automated rules, or by decision, and the person affected usually learns about it at the till.

Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) would go one step further: the issuer of the money itself would sit in the payment path. Cash is the last private payment most people still use, and its share keeps shrinking. This is the subject of Linas K.'s talk at PROOF: "CBDCs, KYC, and the Fight for Monetary Privacy".

Money that loses value by design

The euro, like every national currency today, has no fixed quantity. New money is created by central banks and by commercial banks when they lend. When the amount of money grows faster than the goods and services it can buy, prices rise. That is inflation.

This is not an accident. Central banks aim for prices that rise a little every year, and in difficult years they rise much more. A banknote kept in a drawer buys less each year, by design.

The cost is quiet. Nobody sends you a bill. People who own property or shares are partly protected, because those prices rise too. People who hold their savings in money are not: employees, pensioners, anyone putting a little aside each month.

And the people who decide how much money is created are not the people who bear the cost. You do not vote on it, and you cannot opt out of it inside the system. Piktas takes this further at PROOF in "Why Centralized Money Fails: How Bitcoin Became an Alternative".

What Bitcoin changes

Bitcoin is money with no issuer. There is no company, no central bank and no director. There will never be more than 21 million bitcoin, and that rule, like all the others, is enforced by thousands of independent computers called nodes, run by volunteers around the world. Each one checks every transaction by itself.

Anyone can use it without asking permission: no account to open, no form to fill in. And if you hold your own keys, nobody can freeze your balance.

The name of this part comes from how Bitcoin is secured. To add a page to its history, computers called miners must spend real energy, and the result is a proof that the work was done. Rewriting that history would mean spending all the energy again, which makes cheating too costly to be worth it.

Now the honest part. The price is volatile and can fall hard. Mistakes are irreversible: there is no support desk to call. Bitcoin is a tool, not a promise, and nothing in this book is investment advice.

What you can do about it

You do not need to decide what you think about Bitcoin before you try it. Understanding comes from using it, and it is perfectly fine to use it with very small amounts. A few euros are enough to receive a payment, send one, back up a wallet and restore it. Treat it as a skill to learn, not as a bet to place. Your usual legal and tax obligations still apply.

The six chapters of this part follow the natural order of learning:

  1. Your first wallet and your first sats: install a wallet where you hold the keys, and receive a first small payment.
  2. Backing up your seed phrase: the twelve or twenty-four words that are your money, and how not to lose them.
  3. Lightning and euros in everyday life: instant small payments, and moving between bitcoin and euros.
  4. Privacy in Bitcoin: every transaction is public, so learn what it reveals and how to limit it.
  5. Hardware wallets: a dedicated device for amounts you would not keep on a phone.
  6. Running your own node: verify everything yourself, and stop asking someone else's server.

Start with chapter 11 and go slowly. The only mistake at this stage is to move faster than you understand.
