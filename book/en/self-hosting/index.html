Proof of Autonomy · Chapter 10

Hosting your own services

In brief

"The cloud" is someone else's computer. Self-hosting means running some of your services on a small computer that you own, at home: your files, your photos, your password sync, your notes, later a Bitcoin node. Beginner-friendly platforms now make this about as hard as installing apps on a phone. The price is responsibility: backups and updates become your job. Start with one service, make it reliable, then grow.

The problem

When your files, photos and calendar live in someone else's cloud, that company sets the rules. It can read what is not end-to-end encrypted. It can raise the price, change the terms, close the product, or lock your account by mistake, with nobody to call. Years of family photos then depend on an automated decision and a support form.

Chapter 8 showed how to move to more respectful providers. That is already a big step. But you are still a tenant.

Self-hosting is the next step: the service runs on a machine in your home. The data sits on your disk. There is no subscription, no account that can be suspended, and no third party between you and your files.

It is not free. You pay with time, and you become the person responsible when something breaks. This chapter is about doing it without regretting it.

How it works

A server is only a computer that stays on and answers requests from other devices. You do not need a data centre. An old laptop, a small mini PC or a Raspberry Pi is enough for a household. A mini PC or a laptop with an SSD is usually the most comfortable start. On a Raspberry Pi, use an SSD rather than a memory card, because cards wear out quickly.

On that computer you install a platform: a system that gives you a web page with an app store. You open it from your browser, click "install" on Nextcloud or Immich, and the platform downloads the app and runs it in a container (a sealed box that keeps each app separate from the others).

Your phone and laptop then talk to this box instead of talking to a company. At home this happens over your own wifi. From outside, you need a safe way in, which has its own section below.

What changes most is not the technology. It is that backups, updates and security are now yours.

Choose your platform

All of these are made for people who are not system administrators. They differ in focus, licence and who stands behind them.

Platform Made by Strength Trade-off
Umbrel (umbrelOS) One company, also sells its own hardware The most polished interface, large app store, strong Bitcoin and Lightning apps The code is public, but the licence is not a true open-source licence (no commercial use)
Start9 (StartOS) One company, also sells servers Open source. Built around sovereignty: Tor by default, encrypted backups built in Smaller app catalogue, a little more to learn
YunoHost A volunteer community, non-profit Open source, based on Debian, many apps, one login for all of them Designed for services reachable from the internet, so domains and email appear early. Plainer interface
CasaOS One company (IceWhale) Open source. A simple layer you add on top of an existing Linux system In maintenance mode: the company now focuses on its newer ZimaOS. No built-in backups
Debian or Ubuntu with Docker You Full control, you learn how everything really works, no platform to depend on The command line, and nobody to blame. For the curious

What to host first:

App Replaces Note
Nextcloud Cloud drive, calendar, contacts The classic first app. Keep it simple, do not install every add-on

A reasonable setup for most people: Umbrel or Start9 on a mini PC, with Nextcloud or Immich as the first app. YunoHost is a strong choice if you prefer a community project.

Reaching it from outside, safely

At home, your server is protected by your router: nothing from the internet can reach it. The classic mistake is to "open a port" on the router so you can reach your files from anywhere. It works, and it also exposes your login page to every automated scanner on the internet. Do not do this blindly.

Safer options, from most independent to most convenient:

  • A WireGuard VPN into your home. Your phone opens an encrypted tunnel to your own network and behaves as if it were at home. Open source, nobody in the middle. It needs some router configuration.
  • Tor onion services. Umbrel and Start9 have this built in: your server gets a private .onion address reachable through Tor, with no open port. Slower, but simple and private.
  • Tailscale. Built on WireGuard and very easy: install it on the server and on your phone. Your traffic stays encrypted between your devices, but a company coordinates the connections and the accounts. Headscale is an open-source replacement for that coordination part, which you host yourself.

Whatever you choose, change every default password first.

Step by step

The example uses Umbrel because it is a common first step. The logic is the same for the others.

  1. Pick the hardware. An unused laptop or a mini PC with 8 GB of memory and an SSD is plenty. Connect it to your router with a cable, not wifi.
  2. Install the platform. Download the image from the project's website, write it to a USB stick, start the computer from it and follow the installer. This erases the computer's disk.
  3. Create your account. Open the address shown at the end of the installation from another device on the same network. Choose a long, unique password and store it in your password manager. Turn on two-factor authentication in the settings.
  4. Install one app. Only one. Immich is a good start: install the phone app, enter your server's address, and let it copy your photos.
  5. Set up automatic backups. Follow the 3-2-1 rule: three copies of your data, on two different devices, with one copy in another place. In practice: the server, a second disk with scheduled backups, and an encrypted copy off-site (a disk at a relative's home, or an encrypted cloud backup).
  6. Test a restore. Recover one file from the backup. A backup you have never restored is a hope, not a backup.
  7. Keep it updated. Install platform and app updates regularly. Once a month is a good rhythm.
  8. Only then add a second app. Live with the first one for a few weeks before you grow.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Having no backups. Disks fail, and they do not warn you. In the cloud, someone else handled this. At home, it is you. Set up backups before you trust the server with anything you cannot lose.
  • Exposing services to the internet with default passwords. Automated scanners find open services quickly. Use one of the safe routes above and unique passwords everywhere.
  • Hosting your own email as a first project. Email is the hardest service to run: large providers often reject mail from home servers, and a mistake means lost messages. Use a good provider (chapter 4) and host other things.
  • Making the family depend on it before it is stable. Run it for yourself for a month or two. Move the family photos only when backups and updates have become routine.
  • Deleting the cloud copy too early. Keep your old service in parallel until you have restored from your own backup at least once.
  • Installing twenty apps in the first week. Each app is something to update and secure. Two services you maintain are better than fifteen you forget.
