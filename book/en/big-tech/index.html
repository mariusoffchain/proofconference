Proof of Autonomy · Chapter 8

Replacing Big Tech services, one at a time

In brief

You do not leave Google, Apple, Meta or Microsoft in one day, and you do not have to. Export your data first, then replace one service, live with it for two weeks, and only then move to the next. For every need there are several credible alternatives, most of them open source. Each service you move is one less place where your whole life sits in a single company's hands.

The problem

Most people run their whole digital life inside one or two companies: mail, calendar, photos, documents, maps, videos, all under one account. It is comfortable, and it creates two risks.

The first is the profile. One company that sees your searches, your movements, your inbox and your photos knows more about you than any person does. The second is dependency. If that one account is locked (chapter 6), everything goes at the same moment.

The usual reaction is a burst of motivation: "I delete everything today." It almost always fails. Too many habits break at once, the family calendar stops working, a document cannot be found before a deadline, and two weeks later everything is back where it was, with the added belief that alternatives do not work.

They do work. What fails is the method. Changing one habit is easy. Changing ten at once is not.

How it works

The method has three ideas.

Export first. European law gives you the right to a copy of your data, and the big companies provide a tool for it: Google Takeout (takeout.google.com), Apple's "Data and Privacy" page (privacy.apple.com), and the "download your information" option in the account settings of Facebook and Instagram. The export takes hours or days to arrive. It does two things: you keep your history whatever happens next, and you see how much they hold. For many people this is the moment the motivation becomes real.

Pick one service. Two good ways to choose: the one that knows the most about you (often mail, photos or search), or the easiest one (often maps, notes or search). If you hesitate, take the easiest. An early success makes the next one simpler.

Run both in parallel. Use the new tool for two weeks while the old one still exists. Only when you stop opening the old one do you move the data and delete it there.

Then repeat. Four services a year is a very good pace.

Choose your replacements

The table is organised by need. "Open source" means the code is public and can be checked. "Self-hosted" means you, or someone you trust, must run a server (chapter 10). Everything else is ready to use.

No alternative is an exact copy of what it replaces. Some are better, some ask for a small compromise, and the notes say which one. Try two options for the same need before you decide: they are free or cheap to test.

Need Alternatives Notes
Search See chapter 3 The easiest first step: one setting in your browser.
Email and calendar See chapter 4 for email. Calendars: Proton Calendar, Tuta Calendar, Nextcloud Proton and Tuta encrypt your calendar and publish the code of their apps. Each is one company. Nextcloud is open source, self-hosted or rented from a provider of your choice.
Maps Organic Maps, CoMaps, OsmAnd, Magic Earth All use OpenStreetMap data and work offline. Organic Maps, its community fork CoMaps, and OsmAnd (more detailed, more complex) are open source. Magic Earth is closed source, with some features behind a subscription. None has the live traffic and shop reviews of Google Maps.
Cloud storage and sync Nextcloud, Proton Drive, Filen, Syncthing Proton Drive and Filen are end-to-end encrypted, with open source apps, each run by one company. Nextcloud: see above. Syncthing is open source and has no cloud at all: it syncs your own devices directly, best on computers and Android.
Documents CryptPad, LibreOffice, OnlyOffice CryptPad: encrypted online collaboration, open source, use a public instance or host your own. LibreOffice: open source, on your computer, offline. OnlyOffice: open source, good with Microsoft formats, often used inside Nextcloud.
Notes Standard Notes, Joplin, Notesnook, Obsidian The first three are open source and end-to-end encrypted. Standard Notes belongs to Proton. Joplin syncs through storage you choose. Obsidian is closed source, but your notes are plain text files on your own disk.
Photos Ente Photos, Immich Ente: end-to-end encrypted, open source, paid above a small free plan, can be self-hosted. Immich: open source, self-hosted only, the closest to the Google Photos experience.
Video NewPipe, FreeTube, Invidious, PeerTube NewPipe (Android) and FreeTube (computer) play YouTube without an account or ads. Invidious does the same in a browser through public instances that often break. All three are open source and can stop working when YouTube changes something. PeerTube is different: an open source, federated network with its own videos.
Translation LibreTranslate, the built-in translation of Firefox LibreTranslate is open source and can be self-hosted. Firefox translates pages on your device. DeepL gives better quality but is closed source and receives your text: fine for a menu, not for a contract.
App store on Android F-Droid, Obtainium, Aurora Store F-Droid is a catalogue of open source apps only. See chapter 9.

A reasonable start for most people: search, then maps, then notes, because they need almost no migration. Mail and photos are the ones that matter most, and they deserve a calm weekend each.

Step by step

The example moves your photos. The logic is the same for every row of the table.

  1. Export. Request your full archive from the old service and store it on a disk you own. Check that the files open.
  2. Choose one service from the table. Read its website, check who runs it, whether the code is open, and what it costs.
  3. Run both in parallel for two weeks. Turn on the new photo backup on your phone and leave the old one on. Use the new app every time you want to look at a photo.
  4. Move the data. Import your export into the new service. Most alternatives have an import tool or a guide for the big platforms.
  5. Change the default on every device: phone, tablet, computer, and the devices of anyone who shares the service with you. Turn off the old backup or sync.
  6. Delete the data from the old service, once you have checked that the new copy is complete and that you have a backup of it. Then empty the old service's trash.
  7. Pick the next one. Not the same week.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Purism. Replacing seven services out of ten is a large gain. Keeping YouTube or one work tool does not cancel the rest. All or nothing usually ends in nothing.
  • Moving everything at once. It is the main reason people go back. One service, two weeks, then the next.
  • Forgetting the people you share with. Shared albums, the family calendar, a shared shopping list: talk to the people involved before you move, or keep that one thing where it is for now.
  • Trusting the word "privacy" in an advertisement. Check the facts: is the code open, is the data encrypted so that the company cannot read it, who owns the company, how does it earn money?
  • Deleting before verifying. Open the new copy, count the albums, check the oldest and the newest files, and only then delete the original.
  • Replacing one dependency with another. If everything moves to a single new company, you have a better landlord, not more autonomy. Spread your services across several operators.
