Proof of Work · Chapter 11

Your first wallet and your first sats

In brief

Bitcoin left on an exchange is a promise from a company. Bitcoin in a wallet where you hold the keys is yours. A wallet is a free app, it takes ten minutes to set up, and you can start with an amount smaller than the price of a coffee. Install one, write down the recovery words, receive a few sats, send some back, then try a second wallet to compare.

The problem

When you buy bitcoin on an exchange and leave it there, you do not hold bitcoin. You hold an IOU: a line in the company's database saying it owes you some. As long as the company is honest, solvent, secure and allowed to serve you, the difference is invisible. When one of these fails, it is the only thing that matters.

It has failed before. Mt. Gox, once the largest bitcoin exchange in the world, collapsed in 2014 after losing hundreds of thousands of its customers' coins. Creditors waited about ten years for partial repayment. FTX, one of the largest exchanges of its time, went bankrupt in November 2022 within days, and its founder was later convicted of fraud. Between such events there are quieter ones: frozen accounts, suspended withdrawals, hacked platforms.

Bitcoin users summarise this in one sentence: "Not your keys, not your coins."

How it works

A wallet does not contain coins. Every bitcoin exists only as an entry in the blockchain, the public ledger that thousands of computers keep in sync. What your wallet holds is keys: secret numbers that prove to the network that you are allowed to move a given entry. Spending means signing a message with your key. Whoever holds the key controls the coins. No name, no account, no permission.

The app is therefore only a window and a pen: it shows you what your keys control, and it signs when you decide to pay. Your keys are backed up as a list of 12 or 24 ordinary words. Chapter 12 is entirely about them.

You do not need a whole bitcoin. One bitcoin divides into 100 million units called satoshis, or sats. A wallet works exactly the same with 2,000 sats as with 2 bitcoin, which makes it safe to learn with very small amounts.

Two choices you will meet

On-chain or Lightning. On-chain transactions are written directly into the blockchain: they take from minutes to hours and cost a fee that depends on demand, which suits larger amounts and savings. Lightning is a payment network built on top of Bitcoin: it is instant and costs almost nothing, which suits small everyday amounts (chapter 13).

Custodial or self-custodial. A custodial wallet works like a small exchange: a company holds the keys and shows you a balance. A self-custodial wallet keeps the keys on your phone, and only there.

There is no shame in a custodial wallet. It is the easiest possible start: nothing to back up, nothing to understand, it works in one minute. Treat it like the coins in your pocket: fine for an amount you could lose without pain, wrong for savings. Self-custody asks a little more of you, mainly one serious backup, and gives you in return something no company can freeze, lose or take.

Choose your wallet

All the wallets below are free mobile apps. None is perfect for everyone, and using two from different teams is a good habit: if one project stops, you are not stuck.

A reasonable setup for most people: one simple self-custodial Lightning wallet (Phoenix, Breez or Aqua) for small amounts, and one on-chain wallet (BlueWallet or the Blockstream app) to understand the base layer. Try two and keep the one you find clear.

Step by step

The example uses Phoenix. The logic is the same for the others.

  1. Install it from the official store. Before you tap, check the developer name (ACINQ for Phoenix) and follow the link from the project's official website rather than a store search.
  2. Create a new wallet. No email, no name, no account. This is normal.
  3. Write down the recovery words before receiving anything. Find them in the settings, copy them by hand on paper, and read chapter 12 before adding more than pocket money.
  4. Receive your first sats. Tap Receive and show the QR code. At the conference, someone will gladly send you some. Self-custodial Lightning wallets often charge a setup fee or require a minimum on the first payment: the app tells you, and it is normal.
  5. Send a small amount back. Scan the other person's QR code, check the amount, confirm. It arrives in a second or two.
  6. Check the fee in the payment details. Knowing what a normal fee looks like protects you later.
  7. Try a second wallet, send sats from one to the other, and compare.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Leaving savings on an exchange. An exchange is a place to pass through, not a place to store. Withdraw to a wallet you control.
  • Taking a screenshot of your recovery words. Photos are synced to the cloud and read by other apps. Paper and pen only (chapter 12).
  • Installing a fake wallet. App stores contain copies built to steal. Check the developer name and start from the project's official site.
  • Sending to the wrong network. A Lightning invoice, an on-chain address and a Liquid address are three different things. A good wallet refuses the wrong one. If something looks odd, stop and ask.
  • Starting with a large amount. Make your beginner mistakes with 5 euros, not with 5,000.
  • Trusting anyone who offers to "help" with your wallet online. Nobody legitimate ever needs your recovery words.
