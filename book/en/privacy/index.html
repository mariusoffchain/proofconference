Part 1

Proof of Privacy

Why privacy matters

"I have nothing to hide"

Privacy is not secrecy. You close the bathroom door, you seal your envelopes, you do not publish your salary or your medical file. None of this is suspicious. It is the normal boundary between you and the rest of the world.

Privacy is the power to choose what you share, with whom, and when. When that choice disappears, behaviour changes. People who know they are observed search less, read less, say less. They censor themselves before anyone asks them to. A society where everyone is watched is a society where fewer people dare to disagree, to report abuse, or simply to be different.

"Nothing to hide" also assumes two things: that the rules will never change, and that whoever holds your data will always be well-meaning, competent and never hacked. The history of this region alone is enough to doubt the first. The news of any given week is enough to doubt the second.

What is actually collected

Almost everything you do online leaves a trace: your searches, your location, your purchases, your contacts, the people you write to and the time you do it.

Even when the content of a message is encrypted, the metadata usually is not: who talks to whom, how often, from where. That is often more revealing than the words themselves. A call to an oncologist, then to a lawyer, then to an insurance company tells a story without a single sentence being read. A former director of the NSA put it bluntly: "We kill people based on metadata."

These traces do not stay where they were created. Data brokers combine them into profiles that are sold to advertisers, insurers and political campaigns. And sooner or later they leak. A leaked password can be changed. A leaked date of birth, home address, face or medical history cannot.

What you can do about it

You do not need to disappear, and you do not need to become an expert. Privacy is not all or nothing: every step reduces what is exposed.

Start by asking who you realistically want to protect yourself from. Advertisers and data brokers? Scammers? An ex-partner, an employer, a state? The answer decides how far you need to go. For most people the first two come first, and a handful of habits removes most of the exposure:

  1. a private messenger,
  2. unique passwords and a second factor,
  3. a browser that blocks tracking,
  4. email aliases,
  5. a protected network connection.

These are the five chapters of this part. They are independent: start with the one that looks easiest, not with the one that looks most important. A habit you actually keep is worth more than a perfect setup you abandon after a week.
