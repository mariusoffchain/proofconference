Part 2

Proof of Autonomy

Why autonomy matters

Your digital life is rented

Look at what you own online. Your email address belongs to your mail provider. Your photos sit on a company's servers. Your contacts, your followers and ten years of conversations live inside platforms. You have an account, which means you have permission to use all of this, and permission can be withdrawn.

It happens in ordinary ways. An automated system flags your account by mistake and closes it, with no human to call. A platform changes its rules, and what you published yesterday is no longer welcome. A price doubles. An algorithm changes, and the audience you built over years stops seeing your posts. A service shuts down, as Google Reader and Google+ did, and everything built on it goes with it.

Each time, the loss is bigger than the service itself. If your Google account is closed, you lose your mail, your documents, your photos, and the login to every site where you clicked "Sign in with Google".

None of this requires bad intentions. It is simply what renting means: someone else decides, and you adapt.

Why alternatives to Google and Big Tech matter

A handful of companies now sit between you and almost everything you do: search, mail, maps, the system on your phone, the store where you get your apps, payments, social networks. Most of them are very good at what they do. That is not the problem. The problem is concentration.

When one company carries so much, it becomes a single point of failure: one outage, one hack or one closed account affects every part of your life at once. It becomes a single point of censorship: one decision, made by the company or imposed on it, removes an app or a voice for millions of people. And it becomes a single point of surveillance: one database knows what you search, where you go, who you write to and what you buy.

Using alternatives is not purism, and it does not mean leaving everything tomorrow. Alternatives only exist as long as some people use them, fund them and report their bugs. Each time you choose one, you keep it alive for everybody, and you give yourself something precious: an exit.

Sovereignty is a practical word

Sovereignty can sound like a big political word. In this book it means something simple: the ability to say no, and the ability to leave.

In practice, it rests on three things. You hold the keys. Your identity and your access depend on a secret that only you have, not on an account that a company can close. Your data can move. It is stored in open formats that you can export and open elsewhere, so changing service is an inconvenience, not a catastrophe. Your tools are open source. The code is public, anyone can copy and continue it, so it cannot be taken away from you, even if its authors give up or are bought.

This is what the theme of the conference, "Take back your sovereignty", is about. Not isolation, not distrust of everyone, but the calm position of someone who stays because they choose to, and who could leave tomorrow.

It is not only a matter of comfort. Decentralised, open technology has proved its worth in crises, when central systems fail or are turned against people, and that is the subject of Alexis Roussel's talk at PROOF.

What you can do about it

Not everything, and not all at once. Nobody replaces ten years of habits in a weekend, and nobody needs to. Autonomy is built one service at a time, starting where the dependence worries you most or where the change is easiest.

You also do not have to be absolute. Keeping a Google account for the two things you really need it for, while your mail, your photos and your contacts live elsewhere, is already a very different situation from having everything in one place.

The five chapters of this part go from the idea to the practice:

  1. Keys instead of accounts: how a secret that only you hold can replace a login that a company controls.
  2. Nostr: social media without a company in charge: an identity and an audience that no platform can take from you.
  3. Replacing Big Tech services, one at a time: search, maps, cloud, documents, photos, with several options for each.
  4. Taking back control of your phone: the device that knows the most about you, and what you can change on it.
  5. Hosting your own services: for those who want to go further, your own small server at home.

Chapters 6 and 7 belong together. The others are independent: start with the one that fits your life today.
